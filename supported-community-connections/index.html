Day habilitation · Denver metro

A day out in the world, not a day in a room

Supported Community Connections — SCC — is Colorado’s day programme for adults with intellectual and developmental disabilities. Classes, volunteering, exercise, errands, people. Chosen with the person, not handed to them.

Ask about a place Call (720) 000-0000
The idea

Ask what the day was like, not what the programme provided

There is an easy test for whether a day programme is working, and it is not the activity schedule. It is whether the person comes home with something to tell you about. A person they saw. A thing that went wrong. A joke.

That only happens if the day contained other people who were not paid to be there. So the work is mostly outward: into community centres, libraries, gyms, volunteer rotas, classes, shops, and the ordinary places where a person can become a regular rather than a visitor.

What a week can hold

Six kinds of day, mixed to suit the person

Most people take three or four of these and rotate; nobody does all six.

Volunteering

A regular shift somewhere that needs the help — a food bank, a shelter, a thrift store.

Classes and learning

Community college courses, adult education, art, music, cooking. Some people are working towards something; others just like Tuesdays better with a class in them.

Moving about

Swimming, the gym, walking groups, adaptive sport. Chosen because someone wants to do it, not because exercise is good for them.

Practical skills

Shopping and budgeting, a bus route learned until it is boring, cooking a meal from start to finish, managing an appointment. The skills that shrink the world when they are missing.

Being sociable

The same coffee at the same time with the same people. Meet-ups, groups, events.

Working towards a job

Where someone wants to work, days can be built around getting ready for it — turning up on time, handling a shift, being around colleagues.

Supported Community Connections is one of the services we are approved to deliver as a Program Approved Service Agency, on the Developmental Disabilities and Supported Living Services waivers. Whether it is authorised for a particular person, and how many days, is determined by their Community Centered Board.

How support is arranged

Small groups, or one person and one worker

Groups here are small on purpose. A group of three or four can walk into a community centre and be a few people who turned up; a group of twelve is an outing, and everybody in the room can tell.

One-to-one support is the right call when somebody finds groups overwhelming, when they are building towards something specific, or when the thing they want to do simply cannot be done in a group. Plenty of people have both across a week, and the balance changes as they do.

What does not change is who decides. The person picks what the day is for. Support workers are there to make it possible, not to choose it.

What we plan around

  • What the person already likes, including the things nobody has thought to build on
  • How they communicate, and what helps when they are not understood
  • Noise, crowds, transitions — what makes a place workable or not
  • What they want to be able to do in a year
  • How the day fits with the rest of the household

Getting there

Transport is a separate service and a common reason a good plan never happens. We are approved for Non-Medical Transportation, so the rides can be part of the same arrangement.

How a place starts

From the service plan to a first Tuesday

  1. Talk to the case manager

    Day habilitation has to be on the service plan, and the Community Centered Board decides that. If the plan is due for review, that is the moment to describe what you want the days to look like.

  2. We meet the person

    Not an assessment. A conversation, usually somewhere they are comfortable, about what they like, what they avoid, and what they would do with a free day if anything were possible.

  3. Start with one day

    One day a week, one activity, one support worker. Build from what works rather than from a schedule someone drew up in advance.

What often goes with it

  • Non-Medical Transportation — getting there and home
  • Respite — for the evenings and weekends
  • Personal care at home — mornings and bedtimes
  • A paid family caregiver — for the hours at home
The rest of the week

A good day programme makes the whole week easier

When somebody has three days that are theirs, the other four change too. Parents get hours back. Siblings get a bit of the household attention. The person themselves has something to talk about at dinner.

Because we deliver the day programme, the transport, the respite and the care at home, one family can have all of it without repeating themselves to four different intake teams. Tell us what you are trying to change and we will be straight about what is possible.

Day programme questions

What families and case managers ask

What is Supported Community Connections?

It is the Colorado name for day habilitation delivered out in the community rather than in a building. Someone with an intellectual or developmental disability gets support to spend their day doing things that other adults do — a class, a volunteering shift, a gym, a library, errands, seeing people. It is usually shortened to SCC and it sits on the developmental disability waivers.

How is this different from a day centre?

A day centre is a place people are taken to. Supported Community Connections is a set of activities people are supported to take part in, mostly in ordinary community settings. Some days that means a group of four at a community centre; other days it means one person and one support worker at the volunteering shift they committed to. The measure is whether the day would look recognisable to anybody else that age.

Is it a group or one-to-one?

Both, depending on the person and what the service plan authorises. Small groups work well for people who enjoy company and want a bit of noise around them. One-to-one suits people who find groups hard, or who are building towards something specific like a job or a course. Many people do a mix across the week.

What sort of things do people actually do?

Volunteering at a food bank or an animal shelter. A community college class. Swimming, walking, a gym membership someone actually uses. Learning a bus route until it stops being frightening. Cooking. A regular coffee with the same group on the same day. The activities are chosen with the person, not handed to them, and they change as the person does.

Can someone do a day programme and still be at home the rest of the time?

Yes, and most people do. Supported Community Connections is measured in days or part-days on a service plan, and it commonly sits alongside hours at home, respite for the family, and transport. A young man might do the programme three days a week, have a paid family caregiver for the mornings, and have his mother take a respite Saturday once a month.

How do we get it added to a service plan?

Through the case manager at the Community Centered Board. They coordinate the plan and decide what is authorised after intake and assessment. If a plan is being reviewed, that is the moment to raise it. We can help you describe what you want the days to look like, which tends to be more persuasive than asking for a service by name.

Tell us what a good week would look like

Start with the person, not the service. What do they enjoy, what have they never been offered, and what would you like to be different in a year? We can work backwards from that.
