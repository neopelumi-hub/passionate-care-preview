Respite · Denver metro

A real break for the person who never gets one

Respite is planned time off for a family caregiver, covered by Colorado’s disability waivers. Somebody who knows your relative takes over, and you get an afternoon, a day, or a weekend that is genuinely yours.

Ask about respite Call (720) 432-9703
Why it exists

Nobody is at their best in year eleven of no days off

Families who support a relative with an intellectual or developmental disability are usually doing it every day, for years, without the shift patterns and holidays that any paid job would come with. The tiredness does not announce itself. It shows up as a shorter temper, a missed appointment, a cold that will not shift.

Colorado built respite into its waivers because the state would rather fund a Saturday off than fund what happens when a family reaches the end of its rope.

What it looks like

Four shapes respite usually takes

These are the patterns families most often land on; nothing here is a fixed package.

The standing afternoon

The same few hours every week, in the diary permanently. Families tell us this one changes the most, because it is the only block of time they can plan anything around.

The occasion

A wedding, a hospital appointment of your own, a school event for another child, a funeral. Booked around a date rather than a routine.

Overnight or a weekend

Longer stretches, so a family can actually leave. These need more notice and more planning.

When something falls through

The day programme closes, a sibling is ill, the usual arrangement collapses at eight in the morning. Ring us — we will not always manage it, but we will answer.

How we staff it

Sending a stranger defeats the point

If a parent spends the whole afternoon on the phone checking in, that was not a break. So the work of respite is mostly done before the first session: we sit with the family, learn what the person likes and what they cannot stand, and match them with someone who fits.

After that we protect the match. The same support worker comes back. We introduce a second familiar face early on, so that when the first one is ill you are not meeting somebody new on your worst morning. Direct Support Professionals here are trained for this specific work, not borrowed from a general roster. People who want to do this work can start here.

What we ask about first

  • How they communicate, and what they do when they are not understood
  • What a good day and a bad day look like
  • What genuinely helps when things escalate
  • Food, routines, sensory things, and the non-negotiables
  • Who else should be in the loop

What we will not ask on a web form

  • Diagnoses, medications, or clinical history — those come later, securely, when we speak
Getting respite in place

Three steps, starting with the case manager

  1. Check the service plan

    Respite may already be authorised and unused — it happens more than you would think. The case manager at the Community Centered Board holds the plan and can tell you what is on it.

  2. Name the agency

    Where respite is authorised, the family chooses who provides it. Tell the case manager you would like Passionate Care considered, or ring us and we will explain what they will need from us.

  3. Meet, match, then start small

    We meet your relative on their own ground, agree who fits, and usually begin with a short session while you are still in the house. Then we build up.

We are approved to deliver respite on two waivers: Supported Living Services and Children’s Extensive Support. Whether it is authorised, and how many hours are on the plan, is determined by the Community Centered Board after intake and assessment. We cannot add hours to a plan or promise that any will be approved. What we can do is deliver them well once they are there — and say so straight away if the waiver someone is on is not one we are approved for.

Often on the same plan

  • Supported Community Connections — the day programme
  • Non-Medical Transportation — rides to it and back
  • Residential Habilitation — support in a home of their own
  • Paid family caregiving — for the hours at home
Alongside respite

Respite is usually not the only thing a family needs

A parent who needs a Saturday off often also needs the weekdays to be better. If your relative is at home all week with nothing to do, respite is treating the symptom. A day programme and a way of getting there change the shape of the week itself.

We are approved for all four, so nothing has to be split across agencies. Describe your week to us and we will tell you which combination other families in your position have found works.

Respite questions

What families want to know before they ask

What does respite care actually mean?

It means somebody competent takes over so the usual caregiver can stop. That can be three hours on a Thursday, a whole Saturday, or several days while a family is away. It happens at home or out in the community, whichever suits the person being supported. Respite is a service for the family as much as for the individual, and it is written into Colorado waivers on exactly that basis.

Who pays for respite in Colorado?

For people with a developmental disability waiver, respite is a covered service on the waiver and the hours are set in the service plan by the Community Centered Board. Families sometimes also pay privately for extra hours beyond what the plan allows. What a particular plan authorises is decided by the CCB, not by an agency.

How much respite can we get?

That depends entirely on what the service plan authorises, and it varies a great deal between individuals and between waivers. If you think the amount on the current plan does not match the reality of your week, the conversation to have is with the case manager at the CCB. We can help you describe what a week actually involves, which is often the part families struggle to put into words.

Can we get respite at short notice?

Sometimes, and it depends on who is free. Planned respite booked in advance is far easier to staff well, because we can match the same person to your family repeatedly rather than sending whoever is available. For emergencies, ring rather than email and we will tell you honestly what we can do that day.

Will it be the same person each time?

That is what we aim for, and we build the roster around it. Consistency matters more in respite than in almost any other service: a person with an intellectual or developmental disability should not have to be introduced to a stranger every time their parent needs a break. Holidays and illness happen, so we introduce a second familiar person as backup rather than sending someone new in a crisis.

Does respite have to happen at home?

No. Some families want the house to themselves for an afternoon, in which case the support worker takes the person out — swimming, a walk, a café, a favourite shop. Others want their relative to stay in familiar surroundings while they go out. Both are respite, and the choice is the family’s.

Ask us about a break

Even if you are not sure respite is on the plan, or what the plan says, or who your case manager is any more. We can help you find out.
