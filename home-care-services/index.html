A day, rather than a menu

Three parts of a day, and who is there for them

Nobody needs “personal care”. People need to be up and dressed before the taxi comes, to have eaten something that was not toast, and to get into bed safely. Hours are usually built around those three moments.

  1. The morning

    Getting up, washing or showering, dressing, hair and teeth, the bathroom, a reminder if medication is due, breakfast, and out of the door on time if there is somewhere to be.

  2. The middle

    Lunch, a bit of laundry, the shopping, a load of dishes, a walk, an appointment, and the ordinary business of a household that does not run itself.

  3. The evening

    An evening meal, tidying up, help changing and washing, an evening medication reminder, and getting into bed without a fall.

Personal care

Help with the body itself

This is the part families find hardest to ask for and hardest to keep doing themselves, particularly as a parent ages or a son grows taller than his mother.

Doing it well means the door gets closed, the towel is warm, the person is told what is about to happen before it happens, and they do the parts they can still do themselves.

What it covers

  • Bathing and showering
  • Dressing and undressing
  • Grooming, hair, shaving, teeth and nails
  • Using the toilet, and continence care
  • Help with eating and drinking
  • Moving from bed to chair, and from chair to standing
  • Walking and moving safely about the house
  • Turning and positioning for someone who cannot shift themselves
Homemaker services

Keeping the household from quietly falling apart

The kitchen after three bad weeks tells you more about how someone is coping than any assessment. Homemaker hours exist because a house that has stopped working makes everything else harder, and because a disabled person is entitled to live somewhere clean.

This is not a cleaning service for a whole household. It is the work connected to the person receiving support — their room, their laundry, their meals, their shopping.

What it covers

  • Light cleaning — kitchen, bathroom, the rooms in daily use
  • Laundry, bedding and changing a bed
  • Planning, preparing and cooking meals
  • Washing up and putting away
  • Grocery shopping, and unpacking it
  • Errands: the pharmacy, the post office, picking things up
  • Taking the bins out and other small jobs that stop happening first
How it is paid for and directed

Two questions, and they are separate

Families tend to run these together, which makes the whole thing harder than it needs to be.

Who pays

Usually Health First Colorado, through Community First Choice or through a waiver. What is authorised comes from an assessment by a Case Management Agency.

Who directs

Either your household or us. Colorado arranges that two ways, and which one applies is decided at the assessment, separately from the funding question. It can change later.

We are a licensed Colorado home care agency for personal care and homemaker services, which is what allows us to employ caregivers and bill Medicaid. Nothing on this page determines eligibility or hours — that is the Case Management Agency’s decision.

Also under this roof

  • Respite — a genuine break for the family caregiver
  • Supported Community Connections — the day programme
  • Transport and residential support
If a disability waiver is involved

Home care is often only a third of what a family needs

Where the person receiving care has a developmental disability waiver, hours at home are usually one line on a longer plan. The days still have to go somewhere, the family caregiver still needs to sleep, and somebody still has to drive.

We are approved to deliver those waiver services as well, so one family does not have to assemble a week out of three different agencies. See who we support.

Tell us what the hard part of the day is

Mornings, evenings, the shopping, the lifting, the nights. Start with the bit that is not working and we will tell you what usually helps and who authorises it.
