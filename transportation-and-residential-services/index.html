Non-Medical Transportation

The rides Medicaid’s medical transport will not cover

Colorado Medicaid pays for trips to medical appointments through a separate arrangement. Non-Medical Transportation, usually shortened to NMT, is the waiver service that covers everything else.

It is a common reason good service plans never actually happen. A day programme three days a week is not three days a week if nobody can drive on a Tuesday.

Because we deliver the day programme as well, the ride and the destination can be part of one arrangement rather than two agencies blaming each other at eight in the morning. See what the day programme involves.

Rides it typically covers

  • To and from a day programme
  • To work, or to something someone is training towards
  • Volunteering shifts and classes
  • The shops, the bank, the library
  • Social things — a group, a friend’s house, an event
  • Appointments that are not medical appointments

Non-Medical Transportation is one of the four services we are approved to deliver as a Program Approved Service Agency, on the Developmental Disabilities and Supported Living Services waivers. Whether it is authorised for a particular person, and how much, is determined by their Community Centered Board and written into the service plan.

Residential Habilitation Services

Support for adults living in a home of their own

Residential Habilitation is help with the business of living somewhere, for adults with intellectual and developmental disabilities. We deliver it as Individual Residential Services and Supports — IRSS — which includes host home arrangements. It is not a group home, and it is not a building we run. It is the practical and personal support that makes a home work.

Daily living skills

Cooking, laundry, keeping a place clean, managing money and shopping, personal care where it is needed, and the routines that hold a week together.

Community access

Getting out of the flat. Knowing the neighbours, the bus route, the shop on the corner. Keeping the friendships and the activities that existed before the move.

Health and safety

Medication routines, appointments kept, food that is actually eaten, and knowing what to do when something goes wrong at eleven at night.

Two shapes, and which one fits is a conversation. In a host home, an adult lives as part of the household of a trained provider who has room for them — somebody’s home, not a facility. Other Individual Residential Services and Supports arrangements bring the support to the home the person already has, or to one they share with a housemate. Both are Residential Habilitation on the Developmental Disabilities waiver, and neither one is a group home. Which would suit a particular person, and what is open at the time you ask, is not something a web page can answer: ring us and ask.

For parents thinking ahead

The conversation nobody wants to start

Most parents of an adult with a developmental disability have had a version of the same three-in-the-morning thought: what happens when I cannot do this any more.

The practical answer is that these arrangements are made through a Community Centered Board and they take time, so the families who cope best are usually the ones who started asking early rather than the ones who waited for a crisis. That is not advice about your circumstances — it is just what the timelines look like.

Ring us and ask what the process involves, even if the answer is years away.

Where this is decided

  • Eligibility and the service plan — the Community Centered Board
  • What is authorised and at what level — the service plan
  • Who delivers it — the person and their family
  • What is actually available locally — ask, and ask more than one provider
